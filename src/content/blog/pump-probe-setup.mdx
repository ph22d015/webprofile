A pump–probe experiment asks a simple question: *how does a material respond in the moments after it is hit by light?*
A strong **pump** pulse perturbs the sample, and a weaker, delayed **probe** pulse reads out a change in reflectance or transmittance.
Sweeping the delay maps that change in time.

## The delay line sets the time axis

The delay comes from extra path length. With a retroreflector on a translation stage the beam travels the stage displacement twice, so

$$
\Delta t = \frac{2\,\Delta x}{c}.
$$

A 1 mm stage movement gives $\Delta t = 2 \times 10^{-3}\,\text{m} / (3.00 \times 10^{8}\,\text{m s}^{-1}) \approx 6.67\,\text{ps}$.
The stage resolution therefore sets your finest time step, and its total travel sets the longest window you can observe.

## Things that matter more than they look

- **Spatial overlap.** The probe spot should be smaller than the pump spot so it samples a uniformly excited region. Check both on a camera or with a pinhole at the sample plane.
- **Pointing stability over the delay range.** If the beam walks as the stage moves, the signal changes for reasons unrelated to the sample. Align the stage axis with the beam before anything else.
- **Finding time zero.** The pump and probe must overlap in time at a known stage position; a nonlinear crystal (sum-frequency or second-harmonic signal) or a fast-responding reference sample makes this much easier than searching on the real sample.
- **Detecting small changes.** Pump-induced changes $\Delta R/R$ are often small. Chopping the pump and demodulating the probe signal with a lock-in amplifier at the chopping frequency rejects most of the noise that is not correlated with the pump.

## Why this matters for phase-change materials

In chalcogenide phase-change films the optical constants change strongly between the amorphous and crystalline phases.
Time-resolved reflectance lets you follow that change as it happens, which is what makes questions about switching speed and intermediate states measurable.
