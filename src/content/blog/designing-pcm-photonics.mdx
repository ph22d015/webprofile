Most photonic components are designed once and then frozen. Phase-change materials (PCMs) break that rule: a chalcogenide film can be switched between an amorphous and a crystalline phase, and both phases are stable at room temperature without power.
Because the two phases have very different refractive indices, the same structure can be two different optical devices.

## Start from the simplest structure: one film

At normal incidence the amplitude reflection coefficient at an interface between media with complex indices $N_0$ and $N_1$ is

$$
r_{01} = \frac{N_0 - N_1}{N_0 + N_1}.
$$

Put a film of thickness $d$ on a substrate and the light picks up a phase on each pass through the film,

$$
\delta = \frac{2\pi N_1 d}{\lambda},
$$

and the total reflection becomes (with the convention $N = n + ik$ and time dependence $e^{-i\omega t}$, so that $k > 0$ means absorption)

$$
r = \frac{r_{01} + r_{12}\, e^{2i\delta}}{1 + r_{01} r_{12}\, e^{2i\delta}}.
$$

Switching the phase changes $N_1$, which changes both the interface reflection and the round-trip phase. That is why a single film already gives a large reflectance contrast at the right thickness, and why thickness is the first design variable to sweep. You can try this directly in the [interactive thin-film demo](../../research/#thin-film-demo).

## The real trade-off is loss

A large change in the real index $\Delta n$ is only useful if the extinction coefficient $k$ stays small in the operating band.
Classic Ge–Sb–Te compositions give a large contrast but absorb strongly in the crystalline phase at telecom wavelengths, which is ideal for amplitude modulation and poor for low-loss phase shifting.
Wider-bandgap PCMs such as Ge–Sb–Se–Te, Sb₂S₃ and Sb₂Se₃ trade some index contrast for much lower loss.
Choosing the material is therefore choosing which device you are building.

## From films to metasurfaces

Patterning the PCM into resonators turns a thickness-tuned film into a metasurface whose resonances shift as the phase changes.
Intermediate, partially crystallized states add more than two levels, which is where pulse engineering becomes part of the design.
